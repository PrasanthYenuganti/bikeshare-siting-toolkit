Practitioner input · OPTGIS · PET/COPPE/UFRJ study on bike-share expansion in Maricá

Coverage or transit? What Milwaukee’s Bublr network suggests for Maricá

Prasanth Yenuganti · Data analysis and business systems, Bublr Bikes (Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA) · November 2026 · Public-data edition

A transportation researcher studying bike-share expansion in Maricá, Brazil asked how a city should weigh gaps in station coverage against connections to public transport. My answer, drawn from Bublr’s experience of more than a decade running a docked system: treat them as two decisions at two scales. Coverage and station density decide which neighbourhoods get stations and how close together they are. Transit decides which corner the station stands on. The sections below show the evidence from our own data, the study’s six criteria mapped for Milwaukee, and the pairwise judgments I would give.

01 · SHORT ANSWER

Four rules we actually use

  1. Coverage sets the grid.Bublr 2.0, our current expansion with the City of Milwaukee, aims for a station about every three blocks (≈300 m) in the core and every six blocks (≈600 m) in surrounding neighbourhoods. That grid comes first.
  2. Transit picks the corner.Inside each grid cell we put the station at a bus stop whenever the sidewalk allows. Bublr reports that about 80% of stations sit with a Milwaukee County Transit System (MCTS) stop, and new sites follow the same pattern.
  3. Never place a station alone.A docked bike is only useful if there is somewhere to return it. Our busiest stations sit in clusters. A station beside a strong bus line but far from other stations gets little use.
  4. Equity needs its own lever.We handle affordability with pricing (free income-qualified passes and a sponsored Eco Pass), so siting can focus on reaching neighbourhoods. In a fare-free system like Maricá’s, location is the main equity tool, so the equity criteria should carry real weight.

03 · EVIDENCE

Transit picks the corner

Bublr’s current expansion places stations about every three blocks in the core and every six blocks in neighbourhoods, and puts them at bus stops wherever the sidewalk allows. The public station feed and OpenStreetMap bus stops show how far that has gone.

What this means for siting weights. Coverage and transit point to the same places most of the time (ρ = 0.53 between density and bus stops in Milwaukee; 0.62 in the Maricá study’s cells). Where they disagree, we have found it better to fill a dense neighbourhood with a cluster of stations than to stretch single stations along a bus corridor.
04 · EQUITY

Who lives near a station today

I split the roughly 690,000 residents of our four municipalities into five equal groups by the median household income of their census tract, then estimated how many live within 300 m and 500 m of a station. Coverage is U-shaped: the lowest-income fifth is better served than the middle, and the highest-income fifth is best served, mostly because of the dense, student-heavy East Side.

Today about 389,000 residents live in dense hexagons (1,500+ per km²) more than 500 m from a station. About 221,000 of them already have six or more bus stops within 400 m, and about 166,000 are in the two lowest income fifths. That overlap is where coverage, transit and equity all point the same way, and it is where we would look first.

08 · LESSONS

What I would pass on to Maricá

  • Expand in clusters. Add stations three or four at a time inside one neighbourhood so riders have nearby places to return bikes, rather than one station per district.
  • Use your own trip data before Strava. Operators plan best with their own station-level trip records. App trip data are a complete record of bike-share use; Strava over-represents sport cyclists.
  • Prefer equipment you can move. Our newer battery-powered docks can be rearranged. We downsized 58 low-use stations and reused the docks, which lets a first placement be corrected cheaply.
  • Count delivered bike lanes higher than planned ones. Milwaukee reached its goal of 50 miles of protected bike lanes built or in development, but stations perform once lanes exist, not when they are drawn.
  • Hills and e-bikes. Milwaukee is mostly flat, so slope rarely decides a site for us. Where Maricá’s terrain is steep, e-bikes change the slope penalty a lot.

10 · RESUMO EM PORTUGUÊS

Resumo

Em Milwaukee tratamos cobertura e integração com o transporte público como duas decisões em escalas diferentes. A cobertura e a densidade definem quais bairros recebem estações e o espaçamento entre elas (cerca de 300 m no centro e 600 m nos bairros). A integração define a esquina: colocamos a estação no ponto de ônibus sempre que possível, e 86% das nossas 173 estações estão a até 200 m de um ponto.

Recomendamos expandir em grupos de estações, nunca isoladas. Como o sistema de Maricá é gratuito, a localização é a principal ferramenta de equidade, e o critério de renda merece um teste de sensibilidade.
